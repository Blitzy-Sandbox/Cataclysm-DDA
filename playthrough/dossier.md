# Odette Vachon

*Fifty-two years old. Thirty-one seasons on the water out of Gloucester, the
last nine of them on my own boat. Written at a stranger's kitchen table, in
pencil, before I open the door and find out what is left.*

## Who I was

My name is Odette Vachon and everyone who ever mattered to me called me Dette.
My grandfather came down from Riviere-du-Loup with a duffel bag and a bad
English accent he never lost, and he put three sons on fishing boats out of
Gloucester because it was the only trade he had to give them. My father was the
middle one. I was his only child and there was no son to hand it to, so he
handed it to me, and the men on the dock got used to me in about four years,
which he told me was fast.

I left school at sixteen and I have not regretted it once, though I have felt
the lack of it. I can read weather off the water better than most people can
read a page, and I cannot do long division without a pencil. That trade seemed
fair to me at sixteen and it still does.

I bought the *Marie-Ange* when I was forty-three, secondhand and half rotten,
and I spent two years making her sound. She is forty-two feet of nineteen
seventies fiberglass with an engine I rebuilt twice and she is tied up at a
pier I cannot get to, and I have decided not to think about her for a while.

## What I am like

I am not brave. I want that written down plainly, because I have watched brave
people get killed on the water and I have never once envied them. What I am is
patient and stubborn and very hard to hurry. Thirty-one seasons teaches you
that the sea does not care how much you want to be finished, and that the only
thing that reliably kills people out there is being in a rush.

I do not talk much and I do not like being managed. I have run a two-person
crew for nine years and I know the difference between a person who is thinking
and a person who has frozen, and I have learned to leave the first one alone.

I take my coffee black and I have smoked since I was nineteen and I am not
going to pretend either of those is under control.

## Why I am still moving

My daughter Camille is twenty-six and she teaches fourth grade in Salem, which
is thirty-one miles down the coast from where I am sitting. That is the whole
of it. That is the entire reason I am going to get up from this table.

I am not going to write down what I think has happened to her, because I have
spent my whole working life around men who talked themselves into believing
things about people who were overdue, and it never once brought anybody home.
I am going to go and look. If she is not there I will know that instead of
wondering it.

## What I can do, and what is wrong with me

I am putting this down honestly because I would rather know my own weight than
flatter myself.

I am **strong**, which is thirty-one years of hauling traps and not a gift, and
I am **hard to put down** — I have been thrown against a gunwale in a February
swell and gone back to work the same afternoon. My **eyes are the best thing I
own**; I can see a change in the water at half a mile and I notice things
before I have decided to. I can **carry more than I look like I can**, I am
**comfortable outdoors** in weather that sends other people inside, and I have
a **stomach that has never once betrayed me**, which sounds like a joke until
you have eaten what was available.

Against that: my **back is going**, and has been for six years. There is a
specific movement — lifting with a twist — that will put me on the floor, and I
know it and I still do it when I am tired. I **sleep like the dead**, which was
a mercy in a bunk over a running engine and is going to be a real problem now
that nothing is safe; I have never in my life woken up to a noise. I am
**worse at learning than I used to be** in the sense that anything that comes
out of a book comes slowly, and I know it. And I have an **addictive streak**
that runs straight down my mother's side of the family and has already cost me
one thing I loved.

I am a fisher. That is what I am putting down for a trade, because it is the
true answer and because the tools of it are the tools I actually know: line and
hooks, a knife I have owned for eleven years, and the wristwatch my father wore
until nineteen ninety-one, which keeps time to the second and which I have not
taken off since the day he died.

*Odette Vachon, in pencil, at a kitchen table that is not mine.*
